Finding binders in a library

Fifty known binders hidden among 4,950 decoys matched on molecular weight and logP. A fingerprint model ranks all 5,000, the top 150 are folded against the protein and re-ranked, and the question is whether the folding earns its cost.

Stage one has built and scored all 45 libraries. Stage two has co-folded 5 of 45 targets and set aside 2 whose shortlist held no binder, where neither arm can score above zero. At about 2.6 hours a target on one card this finishes in several days.

No verdict is drawn here. The registration decides the question on an interval over targets and that interval needs the run to finish. We have reported an interim reading as a conclusion twice before and been wrong both times.

How a library is built

Each target has a pool of compounds measured against it in ChEMBL. Those measured at 1 micromolar or better are the binders. They are clustered by chemical series at an ECFP4 Tanimoto of 0.6, whole series go to training or to test, and only test series enter the library. So the prefilter has never seen a library binder, or any close analogue of one.

Fifty test binders are kept, taken whole series at a time, and 4,950 decoys fill the rest. A decoy is a compound measured against an unrelated protein that falls inside the weight and logP window of those 50, so a reader cannot pick the binders out by their size. One percent binders is what a real screening library holds.

Enrichment at one percent is the share of binders in the top 50 of the ranking divided by the share expected at random. An enrichment of 1 is no better than picking at random.

What the prefilter alone does

Across all 45 libraries the fingerprint prefilter reaches a mean enrichment of 13.3x, median 8.0x, and finds nothing at all on 12 of them. The folding stage has to beat that to be worth running.

Every target stage two has finished

A highlighted row is a compound measured as a binder in ChEMBL. The heads column is how far the model's two affinity heads disagree about that compound, printed because the top of a ranking is not stable to that disagreement and a reader should see it rather than take a first place on trust.

Set aside

2 targets hand co-folding a shortlist with no known binder in it, and the prefilter found none in its own top 50 either, so both arms score zero and the difference is zero whatever the folding predicts. They are named in the fourth amendment to the registration, which was written before any of their rows existed because setting a guaranteed zero aside raises the mean difference. Prostaglandin E2 receptor EP1 subtype, Thymidylate synthase.

Can you buy any of this

Mostly not, and we measured it rather than leaving a reader to find out. Forty five compounds sampled from these libraries and looked up in PubChem: 9 of 45 have a chemical vendor, interval 0.11 to 0.34. All 45 are in PubChem, so the lookup found everything it was asked about. One compound in five from a ChEMBL built library is on a shelf and the rest are in the literature.

The top 50 of our own rankings came back at 1 of 15 against 5 of 15 for the decoys. An exact test puts that at p equal to 0.141, so it is a direction rather than a result, and the reason to expect it is that our top picks are recent potent compounds from papers and patents. The measurement is published.

So the screen shows that the pipeline recovers binders hidden in a library. It does not show that the output is an order form. Closing that means screening a purchasable library, which carries no measured affinities, so the result becomes a prospective prediction and a laboratory has to check it.

Every figure here reads from virtual-screen.json for the libraries and virtual-screen-funnel.json for the folded targets. The registration and its four amendments were each written before the stage they affect had run. A retrospective screen recovers binders somebody already found, often made as a series, so a fingerprint can learn the series rather than the binding. The series split reduces that and does not remove it.
