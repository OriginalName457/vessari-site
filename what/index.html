What Vessari does

A laboratory with forty compound ideas and the budget to make five has to choose. We predict how tightly each compound binds the protein, rank them, and put an interval on every number, so the chemist spends the budget on evidence. We have done this for 121 proteins and 2,777 compounds the model had never seen, and every figure on this page is published with the control that tests it.

How it works, in one paragraph

Give it a protein sequence and a list of compounds. For each pair we run Boltz-2, which places the compound and folds the protein around it into a predicted complex. A regression head inside the same model reads that complex and returns a binding affinity, so each prediction arrives as a number with the pose that produced it. It returns a ranked list with a calibrated interval on every number, and it refuses to answer where it cannot give one. The whole thing runs on three consumer graphics cards, not a cluster.

The headline has barely moved as the corpus grew: 4.5 fold at 28 proteins, 4.5 at 41, 4.4 at 121. We used to call that the main reason to believe it. We were wrong. The corpus is not a random sample of proteins. It is the set with enough chemically diverse public measurement to score at all, and we kept drawing from the same families. A number that holds while a filtered sample grows tells you the filter is stable. It does not tell you the method is right.

Our intervals now resample proteins, not targets

Every interval on this site used to resample the 123 targets, which treats them as 123 independent draws. An outside reader pointed out that the corpus holds dihydrofolate reductase from five species. It does: E. coli, human, Candida, rat and Pneumocystis, scoring 0.680, 0.648, 0.607, 0.901 and 0.820. It also holds five chemokine receptors, four somatostatin receptors, four carbonic anhydrases, four sphingosine 1-phosphate receptors, three MAP kinases, three casein kinases and three cathepsins. Twenty one protein names cover more than one target.

We measured the relatedness from the sequences, grouped the targets three ways and recomputed every interval. The point estimates do not move. Median rank correlation stays 0.607 and median error stays 4.6 fold under every grouping.

Grouping by protein name, so the same protein in several species is one draw, gives 87 units and widens the rank correlation interval by 40 percent. The lower bound moves from 0.546 to 0.503. That is now the published interval. A sequence based grouping, which only joins close orthologues, gives 113 units and widens it by 3 percent. Treating all 56 receptors as a single draw gives 63 units and widens it by 52 percent, to 0.498 to 0.662, and we report that far bound without adopting it, because a beta-2 adrenergic receptor and a prostaglandin receptor are different targets.

The old intervals are in the corrections record and the audit is published. Fifty six of the 123 targets are G protein coupled receptors and 102 are human, with 8 rat, 5 mouse, 2 E. coli and one each from Pneumocystis and guinea pig.

The folding model's training set is not a control we own

Our clustering rule controls two things. No two compounds in a held-out set share an ECFP4 Tanimoto cluster at 0.6, and the ligand model we compare against never sees a held-out compound. It controls nothing about what the folding model saw before we started.

Boltz-2's affinity head was trained on 1.2 million measurements across about 2,000 targets and 600,000 compounds, drawn from ChEMBL version 34 and BindingDB, keeping single protein targets with maximum confidence annotations and standard types including IC50 and Ki. Our corpus is 123 ChEMBL single proteins with a median of 730 measured compounds each, and every one of them satisfies those filters. The authors excluded training proteins at 90 percent or higher sequence identity to their own benchmark proteins, which protected their benchmarks and not ours. So our targets are very likely among those 2,000.

The check cannot be run from outside. The training pairs are not released, and an issue on the model's repository asking for them so that overlap can be assessed has had no reply since 27 September 2026. No group using these weights on public affinity data can rule overlap out, and we are saying so rather than letting a reader assume our split handles it. Our audit is published.

There is a measurement that settles what it costs us. The model's authors ran it on eight blinded private hit-to-lead assays from Recursion, hundreds of compounds each, on targets they describe as not accessible to external sources. It reached an average Pearson correlation of 0.39 there, against 0.66 on the public four target FEP+ subset. That gap of 0.27 is the public benchmark premium, measured by the people who built the model.

Our corpus median of 0.607 belongs on the public side of that gap. Our within-window median of 0.271, measured in the 1.5 log potency range a chemist works in, lands beside their private 0.39. Different statistics on different sets, and they agree. The narrow window figure is what this model does on medicinal chemistry data, reproduced from public measurements anybody can fetch and rerun.

Every figure on this site reads from a file you can download, and the files include the ones that were later withdrawn.

What we have measured

A calibrated number where there was none

For a protein with a known sequence and no structure in the Protein Data Bank, the pipeline produces an affinity estimate and an interval that covers what it claims to cover. Nothing else in this project is as well supported as that.

It works on proteins nobody has solved

Twenty one of the 123 proteins we score have no solved structure anywhere. Their median rank correlation is 0.553 against 0.648 for proteins with twenty or more structures, a difference of minus 0.095 with an interval from minus 0.196 to plus 0.221. Fifteen of the twenty one score above 0.5 and none scores below zero. The folding model trains on the Protein Data Bank, so this is the test of whether it predicts or retrieves, and it does not collapse where there is nothing to retrieve. A protein with no structure can still have a close relative in that training data, and separating those needs a sequence identity search we have not run. Sixteen class A G protein coupled receptors were folded from sequence with no membrane and no experimental structure, and scored no worse than the soluble enzymes. That family is a large share of what approved drugs act on and the hardest to get structures for. We expected them to be the point where this stops working and published the prediction before measuring it.

Ranking forty analogues of one scaffold

On 14 published congeneric series, 359 compounds, each series one scaffold measured in one assay from one paper, the median rank correlation is 0.660 with an interval over series from 0.529 to 0.724. Eleven of the 14 score above 0.5. The centred error across all 15 sets is 0.599 kcal/mol against 0.52 for FEP+ on the four sets it covers, at 62 seconds a compound.

These are series and not potency windows. Median pairwise ligand similarity inside a set is 0.569, so the compounds share a scaffold, and the median potency spread is 0.75 log units.

A fingerprint model trained on the same sets reaches 0.567, and the paired difference of plus 0.033 has an interval from minus 0.097 to plus 0.159, so we cannot tell the two apart. What the fingerprint model needs is known actives for that target. We need a sequence.

These proteins carry a leakage control our own corpus does not. The folding model's authors excluded training proteins at 90 percent or higher sequence identity to the FEP+ benchmark, and these series are that benchmark. The result is published.

Direction of change, against a far more expensive method

Across 4,899 within-target compound pairs, it calls the direction of an affinity change correctly 76.8 percent of the time. Free energy perturbation, which costs orders of magnitude more compute and needs hand parameterisation, scores 85.9 percent on the subset where both have an answer. We are worse, by nine points, for a tiny fraction of the cost and with no setup.

What we are measuring next

Beating a cheap two dimensional model on a dense target

On the one target with a full control battery, a random forest trained on known actives alone scores 0.872 against our 0.284, and beats us 60.8 percent of the time trained on five compounds and 97.5 percent on twenty five. Every protein in our corpus has at least 190 known compounds, so every target we have measured sits where that cheaper model would win. We have now tested the regime this method is meant for. We searched all 11,055 single proteins in ChEMBL, found 50 with between 15 and 45 measured compounds, and scored 18 of them. The other 32 held too few distinct chemical clusters to split into a held-out set.

On those 18 proteins we cannot tell the two methods apart. The structure arm ranked better on 7 and the ligand model on 11. The mean difference is plus 0.033 and the interval runs from minus 0.212 to plus 0.274, so it contains zero. The width is 0.486. We registered that rule before we ran it, and it returns unresolved rather than a result either way.

So the claim is not supported and not refuted. What we can still say is narrower. On a protein with no public measurements at all, nobody can train a ligand model, and we can still give a ranking with a calibrated interval. This experiment could not test that, because a protein with nothing measured has nothing to score against. That one needs a laboratory. The full result is in the data section.

Ranking inside the window a chemist works in

A medicinal chemist with forty analogues of one scaffold and potencies spanning a log and a half wants them ranked. Our headline is measured on held-out sets spanning a median of 4.15 log units, where no two compounds share a scaffold, so it has never answered that question. We registered the narrower one and ran it on the predictions already on disk.

Take each target's median measured potency and keep the compounds within 0.75 log of it. Across 84 targets, the structure arm reaches a median rank correlation of 0.271 inside that window and a fingerprint model trained on the same target reaches 0.375. The mean difference is minus 0.1436 and the interval runs from minus 0.2272 to minus 0.0613, which excludes zero, so on this reading a fingerprint model trained on the same target ranks better inside a potency window. The run is complete. 115 targets scored and 8 excluded, six carrying fewer than eight compounds with both a prediction and a measurement and two with no ChEMBL pool. The registered decision rule applies.

On the full held-out sets of those same 84 targets the fingerprint model is ahead by 0.147, with an interval from minus 0.201 to minus 0.096 that excludes zero. Its advantage disappears inside the window. Both arms lose accuracy as the range narrows and the fingerprint model loses more, 0.287 against 0.284.

A narrow window also holds fewer compounds, and that alone moves a rank correlation, so we drew 200 random subsets of each full set at the window's size with no restriction on potency. Those score 0.540. Narrowing the range costs 0.264 beyond what losing compounds costs.

The run continues as the remaining ChEMBL pools arrive, at 84 targets qualifying so far. The result is published and so is the registration.

We know why this number is lower than the 0.660 we reach on published congeneric series. Both ask the model to order compounds inside a narrow potency band, so the task is the same and the measurements are not. A congeneric set comes from one paper with one assay and one protocol. These windows are cut from ChEMBL, which pools IC50 and Ki across many laboratories, and 30 percent of the compounds inside them have their own ChEMBL assays disagreeing with each other by more than our prediction error on that target. On one target we counted 923 compounds measured by IC50 only, 311 by Ki only and 45 by both, with no Cheng-Prusoff correction available for any of them.

We tested the other explanation and it does not hold. A congeneric set differs from a ChEMBL set in two ways at once, narrower potency and one assay instead of many. Across the 60 corpus targets with both arms, the rank correlation between a set's potency range and how the two arms compare is plus 0.096 with an interval from minus 0.157 to plus 0.341, which contains zero, and the narrower half of the corpus shows a slightly larger gap rather than a smaller one. So width is not what separates the two settings and provenance is.

So bring a series measured in your own assay and expect 0.66. Ask for a ranking of compounds pulled from mixed public literature inside a narrow band and expect 0.3, where the limit is the literature.

Finding actives hidden in a library

Ranking a list somebody hands you starts later than discovery does. Discovery starts with a library and a question, which of these thousands is worth making. We registered that screen before building it and amended the registration three times before the expensive stage ran, once because the first design trained the cheap model on the compounds it ranks.

Each library holds 5,000 compounds, 50 of them known actives for the target and the rest drawn from compounds measured against unrelated proteins and matched on weight and logP, so size cannot give the answer away. One percent actives is what a real screening library holds. The actives in the library come from chemical series the cheap model never trained on.

The ranked output is on its own page, one target at a time, with the known binders marked and the model's head disagreement printed beside every compound.

Stage one is finished across 45 targets. The fingerprint prefilter alone recovers a mean of 13.3 times more actives in the top one percent than picking at random, with an interval from 8.6 to 18.7, and it finds nothing at all on 12 of the 45. Stage two folds the top 150 compounds per target and re-ranks them, which takes about four and a half days on this rig and is running now. If both stages enrich equally, the honest product is the prefilter and we will say so. That rule was fixed before either arm ran.

Which single compound to make

The affinity head returns two values for every compound and we record how far apart they are. Across the 2,462 compounds in the corpus the two heads disagree by a median of 2.76 fold about the same compound, with a ninetieth percentile of 13.9 fold and 353 compounds above tenfold. Our published median error is 4.6 fold, so a large part of it is the model disagreeing with itself rather than with the laboratory.

We registered the test and ran it. Setting every compound to one or the other of its two values, ten thousand times per target across 112 targets, the top ranked compound changes in 40 percent of redraws. The interval over targets runs from 35.4 to 44.9 percent and 91 of the 112 targets exceed one draw in five. First place is close to a coin flip.

The list survives better than the winner. Spearman between a redrawn ranking and the recorded one is 0.892, the top five keeps 78.7 percent of itself and the top three keeps 74.7 percent. So the unit we hand anybody is a set of five rather than a first row, and every shortlist now carries the disagreement per compound beside the predicted affinity.

The published interval is wider than this. Its median width is 2.14 log units across the 12 calibrated targets, 4.8 times the median disagreement, and it covers 82.4 percent against a stated 80, so it is not understating this source. What remains untested is replication across seeds, which measures the diffusion sampling and the alignment rather than the choice of head. That run is 17 card hours and it is queued behind the screen. The result and the registration are both published.

Telling you which predictions to trust

A user wants to know which five of forty numbers to believe. We cannot tell them yet. Five candidate signals have been tested and all five were null: the model's confidence in the structure it built, the disagreement between its two affinity heads, noise in the laboratory values, and an arbitrary stereochemical choice affecting a fifth of predictions. The fifth was a model built to predict, before anything is folded, which proteins the ranking will work on, from ten features knowable in advance. Across 121 proteins, leave one out, it does worse than predicting the same number every time, and sorting proteins by its prediction separates the best thirty from the worst thirty by minus 0.005. Every prediction currently carries the same risk, and the result is published.

A fifth signal, stability under molecular dynamics, was withdrawn on 15 October 2026. We simulated a membrane receptor in a box of water with no lipid in it, so the helices had nothing holding them together and that measurement cannot answer the question. It is in the corrections record.

The first bench result

Every result here is retrospective, on measurements that already existed. We tested whether accuracy holds on recently published compounds and could not run the primary test: ChEMBL's affinity data has a median publication year of 2004 and only 38 of 805 compounds were published after 2020. No prediction from this system has been tested at a bench. A first collaboration would close that gap.

Why the negative results are the asset

Anyone can report an accuracy figure. The question a reviewer actually has is whether to believe it. What is published here alongside the numbers:

The sharpest example: the rule that no two compounds in a test set may belong to the same chemical series was documented one way and implemented another, so eight members of one series sat in a thirty compound set. We found it, measured what it cost, published it, and fixed the cause. A reviewer who finds a defect like that themselves discounts everything else. One who sees it found, measured and corrected has reason to trust the rest.

What funding would buy

We do not need compute and we do not need code. We need one laboratory to measure sixty compounds against a protein of their choosing, after we have sealed our ranking and published its digest. Every number on this page was computed on measurements that already existed. A bench result would be the first that was not.

Sixty is a calculation rather than a preference. At forty compounds, a method whose true rank correlation is 0.3 gives an interval from minus 0.011 to plus 0.564, which contains zero. At sixty that interval excludes zero. Forty compounds cannot separate a method that half works from one that does not work at all.

Every number on this page comes from a file in the data section. The things that failed are in findings, the method notes are in notes, and a simulation of two real predictions, one right and one badly wrong, plays in simulation library.
