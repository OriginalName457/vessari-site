What Vessari does

A laboratory with forty compound ideas and the budget to make five has to choose. We predict how tightly each compound binds the protein, rank them, and put an interval on every number, so the chemist spends the budget on evidence. We have done this for 121 proteins and 2,777 compounds the model had never seen, and every figure on this page is published with the control that tests it.

How it works, in one paragraph

Give it a protein sequence and a list of compounds. For each pair we run Boltz-2, which places the compound and folds the protein around it into a predicted complex. A regression head inside the same model reads that complex and returns a binding affinity, so each prediction arrives as a number with the pose that produced it. It returns a ranked list with a calibrated interval on every number, and it refuses to answer where it cannot give one. The whole thing runs on three consumer graphics cards, not a cluster.

The headline has barely moved as the corpus grew: 4.5 fold at 28 proteins, 4.5 at 41, 4.4 at 121. We used to call that the main reason to believe it. We were wrong. The corpus is not a random sample of proteins. It is the set with enough chemically diverse public measurement to score at all, and we kept drawing from the same families. A number that holds while a filtered sample grows tells you the filter is stable. It does not tell you the method is right.

Every figure on this site reads from a file you can download, and the files include the ones that were later withdrawn.

What we have measured

A calibrated number where there was none

For a protein with a known sequence and no structure in the Protein Data Bank, the pipeline produces an affinity estimate and an interval that covers what it claims to cover. Nothing else in this project is as well supported as that.

It works on G protein coupled receptors

Sixteen class A G protein coupled receptors were folded from sequence with no membrane and no experimental structure, and scored no worse than the soluble enzymes. That family is a large share of what approved drugs act on and the hardest to get structures for. We expected them to be the point where this stops working and published the prediction before measuring it.

Direction of change, against a far more expensive method

Across 4,899 within-target compound pairs, it calls the direction of an affinity change correctly 76.8 percent of the time. Free energy perturbation, which costs orders of magnitude more compute and needs hand parameterisation, scores 85.9 percent on the subset where both have an answer. We are worse, by nine points, for a tiny fraction of the cost and with no setup.

What we are measuring next

Beating a cheap two dimensional model on a dense target

On the one target with a full control battery, a random forest trained on known actives alone scores 0.872 against our 0.284, and beats us 60.8 percent of the time trained on five compounds and 97.5 percent on twenty five. Every protein in our corpus has at least 190 known compounds, so every target we have measured sits where that cheaper model would win. We have now tested the regime this method is meant for. We searched all 11,055 single proteins in ChEMBL, found 50 with between 15 and 45 measured compounds, and scored 18 of them. The other 32 held too few distinct chemical clusters to split into a held-out set.

On those 18 proteins we cannot tell the two methods apart. The structure arm ranked better on 7 and the ligand model on 11. The mean difference is plus 0.033 and the interval runs from minus 0.212 to plus 0.274, so it contains zero. The width is 0.486. We registered that rule before we ran it, and it returns unresolved rather than a result either way.

So the claim is not supported and not refuted. What we can still say is narrower. On a protein with no public measurements at all, nobody can train a ligand model, and we can still give a ranking with a calibrated interval. This experiment could not test that, because a protein with nothing measured has nothing to score against. That one needs a laboratory. The full result is in the data section.

Telling you which predictions to trust

A user wants to know which five of forty numbers to believe. We cannot tell them yet. Five candidate signals have been tested and all five were null: the model's confidence in the structure it built, the disagreement between its two affinity heads, noise in the laboratory values, and an arbitrary stereochemical choice affecting a fifth of predictions. The fifth was a model built to predict, before anything is folded, which proteins the ranking will work on, from ten features knowable in advance. Across 121 proteins, leave one out, it does worse than predicting the same number every time, and sorting proteins by its prediction separates the best thirty from the worst thirty by minus 0.005. Every prediction currently carries the same risk, and the result is published.

A fifth signal, stability under molecular dynamics, was withdrawn on 15 October 2026. We simulated a membrane receptor in a box of water with no lipid in it, so the helices had nothing holding them together and that measurement cannot answer the question. It is in the corrections record.

The first bench result

Every result here is retrospective, on measurements that already existed. We tested whether accuracy holds on recently published compounds and could not run the primary test: ChEMBL's affinity data has a median publication year of 2004 and only 38 of 805 compounds were published after 2020. No prediction from this system has been tested at a bench. A first collaboration would close that gap.

Why the negative results are the asset

Anyone can report an accuracy figure. The question a reviewer actually has is whether to believe it. What is published here alongside the numbers:

Published nulls5 candidate signals that did not work, reported rather than shelved
Retracted headline metrics2 including one found by auditing our own test and finding it circular

The sharpest example: the rule that no two compounds in a test set may belong to the same chemical series was documented one way and implemented another, so eight members of one series sat in a thirty compound set. We found it, measured what it cost, published it, and fixed the cause. A reviewer who finds a defect like that themselves discounts everything else. One who sees it found, measured and corrected has reason to trust the rest.

What funding would buy

We do not need compute and we do not need code. We need one laboratory to measure sixty compounds against a protein of their choosing, after we have sealed our ranking and published its digest. Every number on this page was computed on measurements that already existed. A bench result would be the first that was not.

Sixty is a calculation rather than a preference. At forty compounds, a method whose true rank correlation is 0.3 gives an interval from minus 0.011 to plus 0.564, which contains zero. At sixty that interval excludes zero. Forty compounds cannot separate a method that half works from one that does not work at all.

Every number on this page comes from a file in the data section. The things that failed are in findings, the method notes are in notes, and a simulation of two real predictions, one right and one badly wrong, plays in simulation.
